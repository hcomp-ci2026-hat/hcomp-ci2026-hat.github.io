Three position papers and two full papers explore the boundaries, behavior, and evaluation of human–AI collaboration. Read the abstracts below and open each paper to explore the authors’ work.

Paper 01 Position paper

Drawing the Cognitive Automation Boundary in Human-AI Teaming

A position paper on what AI should automate in knowledge work

Jawwad Shadman Siddique

Oregon State University

Abstract

Generative AI makes function allocation feel deceptively simple: if a model can perform an activity faster, the activity appears ready for automation. We argue that capability is the wrong primary criterion for knowledge work. Some activities are epistemically critical because personally reasoning through them builds or exercises the mental models, judgment, and transferable expertise a human will need later; other activities mainly instantiate an already-formed idea. We introduce epistemic criticality, E(a|H), as the value of personally performing activity a for a particular human knowledge state H, and define the Cognitive Automation Boundary as the dynamic line separating cognition that should remain human-owned from execution that can be delegated. We distinguish an epistemic core, an epistemic-support layer, and an execution shell; show why the boundary must move with expertise; and propose a small decision framework combining epistemic criticality, execution burden, and automation reliability. Our position is that Human-AI teaming should maximize useful automation subject to preserving the cognitive operations required for future independent competence, supervision, and learning.

Paper 02 Position paper

Toward a Behavioral Science of Human-AI Teaming

Diagnosing Collaboration Health as a Dynamic Relationship

Manaswi Saha

Accenture Labs

Abstract

Human-AI collaboration is a dynamic relationship—one that forms, deepens, plateaus, and dissolves along observable behavioral trajectories. My position is that the teaming construct, as currently used, is aimed at the wrong unit of analysis to diagnose it. Team cognition, including its explicitly dynamic strands, theorizes the emergence of collective states among members who reciprocate: shared mental models, transactive memory, closed-loop coordination. Yet the two trajectories that most often decide enterprise AI deployments are neither collective nor reciprocal—they are the individual practice of one worker addressed to a partner that does not reciprocate: whether that worker is deepening their practice, and whether they are quietly disengaging. Those belong to expertise-development and technology-discontinuance theory, not to team cognition. To make this concrete I put a candidate instrument on the table: the HAI Behavioral Lens, four telemetry-observable behavioral axes—delegation, steering, abandonment, and deepening. Two map cleanly onto teaming vocabulary; two fall outside it, and they are the two that most often decide whether a deployment holds. That asymmetry has a methodological consequence: the right first question is not “are they a team?” but “is this person working well with AI at all?”—and the right output is not a score but a collaboration health profile read across the axes over time.

Paper 03 Full paper

Teaming Needs Scoreboard: Expert–AI Collaboration in Finance Is Delegation, Not Teamwork

Jing Li, Jinliang Li

Institute for Industrial Innovation and Finance, Tsinghua University

Abstract

“Human–AI teaming” has become the default vocabulary for expert–AI collaboration, and is increasingly used to justify design and procurement decisions in domains far from the crews it was built on. We found that the construct carries three unstated preconditions: jointly attributable outcomes, feedback fast enough to calibrate reliance within the working life of the collaboration, and accountability that can genuinely be shared. We test them against expert financial judgment, a domain that adopts AI advisors at scale and, unusually, audits every decision and every outcome. First, we define the calibration horizon τ, the calendar time before outcome evidence alone can establish that an AI advisor helps, and evaluate it across twelve professions using power analysis and the statistics of Sharpe-ratio inference. It ranges from about a week in flight operations to 196 years for a discretionary equity portfolio manager, while a two-hundred-manager firm running a randomised-withholding programme reaches the same evidence in about four. Identification is available to the institution and not to the individual, which inverts the relation the construct assumes, since its prescriptions are addressed to teammates. Second, we show that the dyad is the limited unit of analysis. When M experts share one advisor, privately optimal reliance cuts each expert’s squared error by 67% while making the consensus 22× worse, and we derive a correlation budget of roughly M/4 independently-erring models. We conclude that in this domain “teaming” describes the work less accurately than it redistributes, in language, an accountability that law makes non-delegable, and we propose delegation under agency, whose four primitives are contractible rather than psychological.

Paper 04 Full paper

Statistical Foundations for Credible Human–AI Teaming Claims

TEAM-Audit: A Two-Endpoint Audit of Margins

Hamed Khosravi1, Xiaoming Huo2

1 Georgia Institute of Technology (H. Milton Stewart School of Industrial and Systems Engineering)
2 Georgia Institute of Technology

Abstract

Human–AI decision systems are often called “teams” when artificial intelligence (AI) assistance improves human performance. That comparison cannot carry the label: a system can help the people using it and still be worse than the AI working alone. We propose TEAM-Audit, a two-baseline audit for the performance version of the claim: evaluate the final, frozen system on data not used to build it; compare it separately with human-only and with AI-only performance; and only when both comparisons are supported with uncertainty call the result performance complementarity — “beats both.” For systems that route cases between a human and an AI, the audit corrects historical logs that record only the acting party’s outcome, and it reports human workload beside accuracy. In simulation, ignoring development-stage uncertainty produced false “beats both” verdicts in 0.504 of null replications, and reusing one sample to both select and evaluate a policy produced 0.103. Reanalyzing a public human–AI study, all 14 assisted conditions beat both baselines when the study’s questions are treated as fixed, but only 2 of 14 when the uncertainty also covers the broader item population those questions stand in for. The verdict turns on a choice that is almost never stated.

Paper 05 Position paper

Cataloger Judgment and Human-in-the-Loop: Artificial Intelligence in Library Technical Services

Jen M. Jones, MLIS

Clemson University

Abstract

Artificial intelligence is increasingly woven into everyday life and has become difficult to avoid or ignore. In library technical services, however, its value depends on the work it is asked to do, the evidence available for review, and the professional judgment surrounding its use. Building on this context, this chapter examines recent literature on AI in metadata creation, cataloging support, quality control, with attention to both practical applications and ongoing limitations. Furthermore, it presents local exploratory work involving a Catalog Buddy developed through iterative MARC review and a rule-guided quality-control project for historical photograph metadata. In both projects, documented methods were used to keep source evidence, local practice, and cataloger decisions visible. As a result, AI proved most useful for structured comparison, recurring patterns, and triage, while it was less dependable when the work required item-specific context, authority research, subject analysis, classification, or knowledge of a local collection. Importantly, neither project supports autonomous cataloging. Instead, the findings demonstrate how carefully scoped tools can assist review while responsibility remains with trained staff. Finally, questions of bias, privacy, transparency, labor, and public description are treated as part of the workflow itself and not as concerns to be considered only after adoption.
